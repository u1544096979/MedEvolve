\begin{abstract}
Generating high-quality tasks for medical agents from longitudinal EHRs is challenging because patient records differ in the clinical relationships they support, while available tools restrict how evidence can be retrieved. Here, we propose MedEvolve, a framework that improves medical task generation at two complementary levels, task grounding and strategy evolution. At the task-construction level, dual exploration distills recurring clinical-temporal patterns and tool-access constraints into reusable guidance. At the strategy level, feedback-driven evolution then converts verification and independent-solving outcomes into cross-patient construction rules and task-direction preferences, rather than only repairing individual questions.
MedEvolve achieves higher verified higher-order task yield than the baselines on both benchmarks. Adding its synthetic tasks improves official-test success rates by 6 and 2 percentage points for ACE and Reflexion, respectively.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Longitudinal electronic health records (EHRs) document diagnoses, tests, and treatments across encounters and are a primary source of medical agent tasks. MedAgentBench and PhysicianBench evaluate LLM agents in such environments~\citep{jiang2025medagentbench,liu2026physicianbench}, but task design and review typically rely on clinical experts, limiting scalability. Among automatic generation methods, TaskCraft constructs atomic tasks from document content, verifies them, applies depth and width expansion, and optimizes few-shot prompt configurations using verification pass rates~\citep{shi2025taskcraft}. CuES and AgentEvolver synthesize tasks from environment interaction trajectories and check feasibility through execution~\citep{mai2025cues,zhai2025agentevolver}. These approaches construct verifiable tasks from documents or environment interactions. We further examine clinical events, temporal relationships, and evidence patterns in longitudinal records across patients, and use verification and independent-solving feedback to update generation guidance and task-direction weights across patients.

Task generation from longitudinal records faces two challenges. First, task validity depends on both patient records and tool capabilities. A single trajectory does not reveal recurring clinical relationships or evidence distributions across patients, and complete trajectories may exceed the input budget. Whether the required evidence is retrievable must also be checked through tool interaction. Second, different cases support different task types and reasoning demands, so generation guidance needs to incorporate construction issues revealed by evidence verification and independent solving.

We propose MedEvolve, which combines data and environment exploration with feedback-driven strategy updates across patients, without changing model parameters. Our contributions are: (1)~grounding task generation in clinical event patterns and tool-access constraints; (2)~using evaluation feedback to revise natural-language guidance and task-direction weights; and (3)~evaluating valid higher-order task yield in two EHR environments and the downstream value of synthetic tasks for experience learning.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Problem Setting}

Given a medical environment $\mathcal{E}$ and a set of longitudinal patient trajectories $\mathcal{D}=\{X_p\}$, the goal is to construct a medical agent task for each trajectory $X_p$. Each task consists of an instruction $q$, a candidate reference answer $a$, and source evidence $e$. Generation is controlled by a strategy $\theta_r=(g_r,w_r)$, where $g_r$ is natural-language generation guidance, $w_r$ holds sampling weights over task types and difficulty directions, and $r$ is the evolution round. MedEvolve first obtains data guidance $K_D$ and environment guidance $K_E$ through exploration, then updates $\theta_r$ using evaluation feedback. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} shows the overall pipeline.

\subsection{Data and Environment Exploration}

A single patient's trajectory shows only which records that case contains, which makes it hard to tell what pattern those records follow across the whole data source, and reading a large number of patients again at every generation step is infeasible. We therefore run data exploration before generation and organize cross-patient observations into reusable guidance.

\textbf{Data exploration} targets longitudinal patient trajectories. The exploration agent reads the records of multiple patients, analyzes how the data are organized, how they change over time, how clinical events relate to one another, and how evidence is distributed, and distills the information patterns required by different task types into data guidance $K_D$. $K_D$ describes only reusable patterns and contains no patient-specific clinical facts.

\textbf{Environment exploration} targets the environment interface. The exploration agent actually calls read-only query tools and records access methods, return structures, and interaction constraints into environment guidance $K_E$, which serves to judge whether the evidence a question requires can be obtained through the tools.

Both explorations are completed during initialization and remain unchanged in all subsequent rounds.

\subsection{Task Generation and Evaluation}

For patient $X_p$, the generator conditions on $K_D$, $K_E$, and the current strategy $\theta_r$, and generates $(q,a,e)$ according to the specified task type and difficulty direction, where key facts carry references to the original records.

Candidate tasks then pass through verification, scoring, and independent solving in sequence. Verification re-reads the original records and checks that the evidence claims support the reference answer; tasks that pass verification are then assessed for task quality and reasoning depth and answered independently by a fixed agent in the environment. The solving agent has no access to reference answers or evidence claims.

\subsection{Feedback-Driven Strategy Updates}

After each round, the system aggregates that round's verification defects, quality and reasoning scores, and solve traces, and attributes them into feedback $F_r$. Attribution must separate defects of the task itself (such as factual errors, insufficient evidence, or ambiguous questions), the genuine reasoning difficulty the task poses, and model, tool, or environment failures, so that execution anomalies are not mistaken for task difficulty. The strategy is updated as
\begin{equation}
\theta_{r+1}=\mathcal{U}(\theta_r,F_r),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{U}$ is implemented by an LLM. The update acts on two parts: it writes generalizable construction experience into $g_{r+1}$, adjusting the selection of clinical information, the organization of evidence, and task design; and it adjusts $w_{r+1}$ according to the quality and difficulty performance of each direction, changing the direction distribution of subsequent tasks.

Different rounds use different patient batches to generate tasks. After the scheduled rounds the strategy is frozen and evaluated on a held-out patient set.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Evaluation Conditions and Metrics}

\textbf{Environments and comparison conditions.} We use 108 PhysicianBench patients and 100 MedAgentBench patients with clinical records, reserving 81 and 75, respectively, for held-out evaluation and the remainder for strategy evolution. The sets are patient-disjoint. All methods share the held-out patients and generation model, GPT-5.4-nano, and generate at most two read-only tasks per patient, yielding fixed totals of 162 and 150 requested tasks. Patient splits and information-access rules appear in Appendix~\ref{app:env}.

\textbf{Metrics.} Tasks are verified against source records. GPT-4o-mini and GPT-5.4-nano independently assess the minimum reasoning required, with scores aggregated into levels 1--4. Verified tasks at level 3 or above count as higher-order (HO). Our primary metrics are HO count and yield, the latter dividing HO count by the fixed requested-task total and thus accounting for generation failures and invalid tasks. Mean level across all verified tasks with difficulty scores is an auxiliary metric. Appendix~\ref{app:metrics} details scoring and aggregation.

\subsection{Experiment 1: Generating Valid Higher-Order Tasks}

We compare generation methods under a shared patient set and task-count limit. SingleShot generates in one pass; Workflow uses localization, generation, and filtering; SelfRefine~\citep{madaan2023selfrefine} revises candidates through self-feedback; TaskCraft applies depth and width expansion. EnvExplore uses environment guidance with a fixed initial strategy, whereas MedEvolve combines data and environment exploration with the full strategy after two feedback-update rounds. Computational costs differ across methods; the comparison controls patients and task counts. Appendix~\ref{app:methods} describes procedures and budgets.

MedEvolve achieves the highest HO yield in both environments (Table~\ref{tab:exp1}), with a larger margin over the closest baseline, EnvExplore, on MedAgentBench: 13 additional tasks and an 8.6-percentage-point gain, compared with two tasks and 1.2 points on PhysicianBench. The full framework therefore leads in this evaluation, although the PhysicianBench margin is small.

Verification rate and HO yield capture different task properties. TaskCraft produces no HO tasks and has a mean level near 1. MedEvolve has a lower verification rate than some baselines but retains the most valid HO tasks (Appendix~\ref{app:counts}); verification rate alone does not capture reasoning demands. Mean level also requires attention to sample size: Workflow slightly exceeds MedEvolve on PhysicianBench (2.30 vs.\ 2.27), based on only 10 verified tasks.

\subsection{Experiment 2: Supplementing Official Training Tasks with Synthetic Tasks}

We test whether synthetic tasks supplement official tasks for experience learning. In PhysicianBench, ACE~\citep{zhang2026ace}, Reflexion~\citep{shinn2023reflexion}, and OpenClaw~\citep{openclaw2026} each learn under two settings: \emph{Official 20} uses 20 official tasks; \emph{Official + Synthetic 40} adds 20 verified MedEvolve tasks to those same tasks. Both settings use Qwen3.8-27B with fixed model parameters. Experience is frozen after training, and both settings are evaluated on the same 50 official test tasks. Reference answers are used only for scoring. Appendix~\ref{app:exp2} details framework mechanisms and reproducibility settings.

\raggedbottom

\section{Conclusion}

We present MedEvolve, which combines data and environment exploration to ground medical agent task generation in longitudinal patient records and tool-based evidence access. Verification and independent-solving feedback update generation guidance and task-direction weights across patients, without updating model parameters.

On PhysicianBench and MedAgentBench, MedEvolve achieves the highest valid higher-order task yield among the six compared methods, producing 23 and 77 verified higher-order tasks, respectively. Downstream experience-learning experiments further suggest potential value for synthetic tasks in some frameworks: adding synthetic tasks improves official-test success rates for ACE and Reflexion, but reduces the rate for OpenClaw, indicating differences in benefits across frameworks. These findings support dual exploration as an approach to constructing higher-order medical agent tasks and provide an empirical basis for studying how synthetic tasks interact with experience-learning mechanisms.

\section*{Limitations}

The downstream experience-learning experiment compares training on 20 official tasks with training on those tasks plus 20 synthetic tasks, changing both task count and composition. Controls using an equal number of official tasks or tasks produced by other generation methods would help distinguish the effects of training-set size from those of synthetic-task characteristics. The current downstream results come from a single seed and 50 test tasks in PhysicianBench; the stability of the gains and their performance in other environments require further evaluation.

MedEvolve derives generation guidance from patient records and tool interfaces within a particular environment. The current held-out patient evaluation examines strategy transfer within that environment. The applicability of existing guidance, and the adaptations it requires when healthcare institutions, record structures, or evidence-retrieval tools change, have not yet been systematically evaluated.

\subsection{Models and Baseline Adaptation}
\label{app:methods}

In Experiment 1, GPT-5.4-nano is used for generation, independent solving, verification, and completion assessment. GPT-4o-mini and GPT-5.4-nano judge difficulty. All six methods share the held-out patients, a limit of two tasks per patient, and the common evaluation protocol.

TaskCraft adapts patient records and read-only FHIR tools to conclusion extraction, atomic task generation, verification, and depth/width expansion. The two expansion branches start independently and share the generation budget. Workflow performs localization, generation, and filtering; SingleShot generates in one pass. SelfRefine uses at most three self-reviews and two revisions, stopping early upon acceptance. EnvExplore uses environment guidance and a fixed initial strategy. MedEvolve uses dual exploration and the full strategy after two update rounds. Internal call counts differ, so the comparison does not control for equal computational expenditure.

\subsection{Downstream Experience Learning and Official Evaluation}
\label{app:exp2}

The execution model is Qwen3.8-27B with UD-Q4\_K\_M quantization, thinking disabled, a budget of 100 model calls per task, and seed 42. Both settings start from the same initial state and share 20 official training tasks. The mixed setting adds verified MedEvolve tasks: 10 generated by GPT-4o-mini and 10 by GPT-5.4-nano. Experience is frozen after training, and both settings are scored on the same 50 official test tasks using PhysicianBench's native checkpoints. Reference answers are used only for scoring.

ACE updates a strategy playbook; Reflexion stores reflections and transferable experience; OpenClaw updates skills through its native Skill Workshop. Reflexion retrieves at most three relevant experiences per task and stores no patient identifiers, individual clinical values, or task-specific answers. Experience is not updated during testing, and scores do not trigger reflection or retries.

Test success rates use all 50 planned tasks as the denominator. ACE completes scoring for 48 tasks in the official-only setting and 50 in the mixed setting; both settings for Reflexion and OpenClaw cover all 50 tasks.
